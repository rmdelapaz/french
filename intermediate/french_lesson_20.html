🇫🇷 Lesson 20: Idioms & Expressions Figées

You can now build any sentence French grammar allows. What still marks you as a learner is that your French is literal. Native speakers reach constantly for expressions figées — fixed phrases whose meaning has nothing to do with their parts. Avoir un poil dans la main is not about hair. It means you are bone idle.

💡 The rule for all idioms

Learn them whole, never word by word. And learn them with their register — an idiom that delights your friends can be badly wrong in an interview.

🔍 Guessing an Idiom You Have Never Met

You will meet unfamiliar ones constantly. Three tactics:

TacticApplied to « Il a pris ses jambes à son cou. »
Read the whole situation, not the phraseSomeone was frightened and the next sentence says he was gone.
Look for the emotional colourLegs + neck + sudden = urgency, escape.
Accept an approximate meaning and move on"He ran for it." Close enough to follow the story.

Never translate the parts and hope. Prendre ses jambes à son cou literally means "to take one's legs to one's neck", which tells you nothing at all.

🇫🇷 France

Most of the idioms above are pan-francophone and safe in France. Regional colour exists too — the south adds its own (péguer for sticky), and du coup is the defining verbal tic of contemporary French speech, to the point of parody.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québec has a rich parallel stock, much of it weather- or winter-flavoured: attache ta tuque ("hold on tight", literally "tie your hat on"), il fait frette (it's freezing), avoir de la misère à (to struggle to), c'est de valeur (that's a shame), se pogner le beigne (to laze about), être dans le jus (to be snowed under). And beware écœurant: with a grimace it means disgusting, with a grin it means fantastic.
