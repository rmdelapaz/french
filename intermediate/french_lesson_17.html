🇫🇷 Lesson 17: Argot, Verlan & Screen French

Lesson 16 placed familier at the bottom of the register ladder. Below it sits argot — slang — and inside argot, French has something genuinely unusual: verlan, a system for manufacturing new words by turning old ones inside out. You will not need to produce much of this. You will absolutely need to understand it.

🔄 How Verlan Works

Split a word into syllables and reverse them. The name is the method applied to itself: l'envers ("the reverse") → vers-l'en → verlan.

💡 Verlan ages — and gets re-verlanised

When a verlan word becomes mainstream, it loses its edge and gets flipped a second time. Arabe → beur (1980s, now completely standard — there was a radio station called Beur FM) → reubeu. Likewise femme → meuf → feumeu. If a verlan word sounds dated to you, it probably is.

⚠️ When to Use It — and When Not To

🚫 The safe rule

Understand everything; produce very little. Slang used by a non-native too early sounds like someone trying on a costume. Wait until someone uses it with you, then mirror it back cautiously. Never use it with strangers, in any workplace, or in writing.

Three practical cautions. Some argot is much cruder than its dictionary gloss suggests — check before repeating. Verlan is generationally and geographically marked, so a word that works in the Paris suburbs may be meaningless in Bordeaux or Brussels. And wesh, which you will hear constantly in films, is very strongly marked — recognise it, do not deploy it.

🇫🇷 France

Verlan grew out of the working-class banlieues around Paris and Marseille, spread through rap and cinema (La Haine is the classic text), and some words have entered the standard language entirely — few French people now register meuf or beur as slang at all. The Larousse lists them.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québec has essentially no verlan — it is a French-from-France phenomenon. Québécois slang draws on other sources: English (c'est le fun, checker, être badloqué), older French dialects, and its own inventions — un chum (boyfriend/mate), une blonde (girlfriend), capoter (to freak out), écœurant (which confusingly means both "disgusting" and "fantastic", depending on tone). Slang is one of the places where the two varieties diverge most sharply.
