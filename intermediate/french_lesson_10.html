🇫🇷 Lesson 10: Past-Participle Agreement with Avoir

At A2 you learned that être verbs agree with their subject: elle est partie. You were also told that avoir verbs never agree. That was a useful simplification — and it was not quite true. This lesson gives you the real rule. It is one of the most distinctively French pieces of grammar there is, and it appears in every DELF written paper.

📏 The Rule, In One Line

💡 The whole thing

With avoir, the past participle agrees with the direct object — but only if that object comes before the verb.

Most of the time the direct object comes after, so nothing happens. That is why the A2 simplification works so well:

J'ai vu les filles. — object after the verb → no agreement.
Je les ai vues. — object before the verb → agreement.

🎯 The Three Ways an Object Gets in Front

There are exactly three. Learn to spot them and the rule becomes mechanical.

1. A direct object pronoun: le, la, les, me, te, nous, vous

FrenchPronunciationMeaning
Je les ai vues.zhuh lay zay VÜI saw them. (f. pl.)
Il l'a prise.eel lah PREEZHe took it. (f. sg.)
Nous vous avons attendus.noo voo zah-vohn ah-tahn-DÜWe waited for you. (m. pl.)
Elle m'a invitée.el mah an-vee-TAYShe invited me. (speaker is female)

2. The relative pronoun que

Que is by definition a direct object, so a participle after it agrees with whatever que refers back to.

La lettre que j'ai écrite · Les films que nous avons vus · Les années qu'il a vécues.

Contrast with qui, which is a subject and triggers nothing of the kind: la lettre qui est arrivée agrees for a different reason entirely (it is an être verb).

3. A question word carrying the object

Quelle robe as-tu choisie ? · Combien de lettres as-tu écrites ? · Lesquels avez-vous pris ?

🚫 What Does Not Trigger Agreement

CaseExampleWhy not
Indirect objectJe leur ai parlé.Parler à — indirect, so never agrees.
The pronoun enDes pommes ? J'en ai pris deux.En is partitive; agreement is blocked.
Object still after the verbJ'ai vu les filles.Nothing precedes.
Impersonal verbsLa chaleur qu'il a fait.No real subject, no real object.

⚠️ The COD/COI test

Before you agree, check whether the verb takes a direct object at all. Téléphoner, parler, répondre, plaire, obéir and ressembler all take à — their objects are indirect, so je leur ai téléphoné never gains an -s, however plural they are.

🪞 Reflexive Verbs — The Same Rule in Disguise

Reflexives take être, but they do not follow the être rule. They follow the avoir rule, with se as the preceding object. So the question is always: is that se direct or indirect?

FrenchAgreement?Why
Elle s'est lavée.✅ yesse = direct object (she washed herself)
Elle s'est lavé les mains.❌ noles mains is the direct object, and it follows
Ils se sont vus.✅ yesvoir quelqu'un — direct
Ils se sont parlé.❌ noparler à quelqu'un — indirect
Elle s'est demandé pourquoi.❌ nodemander à — indirect
Les mains qu'elle s'est lavées.✅ yesque now precedes the verb

That third-from-last row is the one to remember: elle s'est lavé les mains has no agreement, but move the hands in front with a relative pronoun and the agreement comes back. The rule is completely consistent — only the word order changed.

👂 Does Anyone Actually Hear It?

Usually not — and this is worth knowing, because it explains why native speakers get it wrong too. Vu and vues sound identical. The agreement is audible only when the participle ends in a consonant that the -e brings to life:

ParticipleMasculineFeminineAudible?
vuvuvue❌ identical
faitfait "fay"faite "fet"✅ yes
mismis "mee"mise "meez"✅ yes
écritécrit "ay-kree"écrite "ay-kreet"✅ yes
prispris "pree"prise "preez"✅ yes

So in speech the rule matters for a handful of verbs. In writing it matters every single time — which is exactly why examiners love it.

🇫🇷 France

This is a genuine sore point for native speakers: the participle agreement is one of the most-failed items in the certificat Voltaire, the French spelling certification that employers increasingly ask for. Reform has been proposed repeatedly and rejected each time. Getting it right marks you out as a careful writer.

🇨🇦 Canada

Identical rules — this is codified grammar, shared by both standards. Québec schools drill it the same way, and Québécois professional writing holds to it just as firmly. If anything, the audible cases (faite, prise, mise) stand out more in Québécois speech, where final consonants are often articulated a touch more clearly than in Paris.
