🇫🇷 Lesson 9: La Grammaire du Français Parlé

B2 lesson 16 gave you the register ladder and told you the bottom rung existed. This lesson teaches its grammar — because spoken French is not written French pronounced badly. It is a consistent system with its own question forms, its own word order, its own negation and its own set of structural particles. Transcribe a real conversation and it looks like a list of errors. It is not.

Learning this does two things. It makes fast speech comprehensible. And it lets you produce French that sounds like a person rather than a textbook — while still being able to write the other version, which is what register control means.

Two patterns cover most of it: subject pronouns lose their vowel or their consonant (je → j’/ch, tu → t’, il → i’), and il y a collapses to y’a. Add the dropped ne below and you can decode most casual speech.

❓ Questions as They Are Actually Asked

You learned three question forms at A2: intonation, est-ce que, inversion. Real speech overwhelmingly uses a fourth — the question word stays where the answer would go.

Spoken (in situ)Written / formal
Tu vas où ?Où allez-vous ?
Il arrive quand ?Quand arrive-t-il ?
Tu fais quoi ?Que faites-vous ?
C’est combien ?Combien cela coûte-t-il ?
Tu pars avec qui ?Avec qui partez-vous ?
Pourquoi tu dis ça ?Pourquoi dites-vous cela ?

Note quoi rather than que: que cannot stand at the end of a clause, so spoken French uses the stressed form. Tu fais quoi ? is normal; tu fais que ? is impossible. And pourquoi is the one question word that still prefers the front even in speech.

🎯 Dislocation as the Default

Lesson 2 introduced dislocation as an emphasis device. In speech it is not a device — it is the ordinary way to organise information. The topic goes outside the clause, and a pronoun holds its place inside.

Mon frère, il habite à Lyon.
Le film, je l’ai trouvé nul.
Ma sœur, son mari, il est prof. — double dislocation, perfectly normal.
Il est où, ton sac ? — dislocation to the right.
Moi, les maths, j’ai jamais compris. — two topics, one clause.

💡 Why this matters for listening

If you are waiting for a subject to be followed by its verb, a dislocated sentence throws you: you hear a noun, then a pause, then what sounds like a fresh sentence with a pronoun. Expect it and the sentence resolves instantly.

🚫 Dropping ne

In ordinary conversation the ne of ne… pas simply is not there. Pas is the negator; ne is a written formality.

SpokenWritten
Je sais pas.Je ne sais pas.
C’est pas grave.Ce n’est pas grave.
J’ai rien dit.Je n’ai rien dit.
Y’a personne.Il n’y a personne.
Faut pas exagérer.Il ne faut pas exagérer.

Where ne survives in speech: fixed expressions (n’importe quoi, ne serait-ce que), the ne explétif after craindre, and deliberate emphasis. Where it never disappears: anything written above the register of a text message.

⚠️ Asymmetric advice

Dropping ne in speech makes you sound normal. Dropping it in writing makes you look uneducated. This is one of the few places where the spoken and written norms genuinely diverge and both are compulsory.

🔁 Repairs and Turn-Taking

Real speech is built and rebuilt as it goes. The repair devices are conventional:

Je veux dire… · Non, attends… · Enfin, je veux dire… · C’est-à-dire que… · Comment dire… · Bref.

And French turn-taking overlaps. Interrupting a friend mid-sentence is participation, not rudeness; waiting for a full stop can read as disengagement. In a professional setting, the formulas of lesson 18 (je me permets de vous interrompre) do the same job politely.

🔄 The Two-Way Skill

SpokenWritten
Y’a pas de souci, j’vais l’faire.Il n’y a aucun problème, je vais le faire.

Being able to move a sentence between those two columns, in both directions, is exactly what the CEFR means by C1 register control. It is also the DALF oral examiner's favourite thing to observe.

🇫🇷 France

Du coup has become the signature consequence marker of French under forty, to the irritation of everyone over sixty; genre plays the role English like plays. In situ questions are so dominant that inversion in casual speech can sound affected. Regional speech adds its own tags: hein everywhere, quoi heavily in the west, té and ô in the south.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québécois spoken grammar has features France lacks entirely. The interrogative particle -tu turns any statement into a yes/no question: Ça se peut-tu ? · T'es-tu prêt ? — nothing to do with the pronoun tu. Fait que (often fa que) is the consequence marker where France says du coup. Là is appended constantly as a discourse marker (moi là, je pense que…), and tu is used far more readily with strangers than in France.

✍️ Practice

Exercise 1 — Expand to the written form

  1. chuis fatigué → _____ suis fatigué (Answer: je)
  2. y’a personne → il n’_____ a personne (Answer: y)
  3. t’as vu ? → _____ as vu ? (Answer: tu)
  4. j’sais pas → je _____ sais pas (Answer: ne)
  5. faut qu’on parte → il _____ que nous partions (Answer: faut)

Exercise 2 — Ask it in situ

Rewrite the question word where the answer would go.

  • Où vas-tu ? → Tu vas _____ ? (Answer: où|ou)
  • Que fais-tu ? → Tu fais _____ ? (Answer: quoi)
  • Quand arrive-t-il ? → Il arrive _____ ? (Answer: quand)
  • Avec qui pars-tu ? → Tu pars avec _____ ? (Answer: qui)

Exercise 3 — Which particle?

From: du coup · quoi · hein · en fait · genre.

  1. Il a raté son train, _____ il est arrivé en retard. (Answer: du coup)
  2. C’est pas très clair, _____ ? (seeking agreement) (Answer: hein)
  3. Je croyais qu’il venait ; _____ , il était malade. (Answer: en fait)
  4. Ça coûte _____ trente euros. (approximation) (Answer: genre)
  5. C’est compliqué, _____ . (closing the point) (Answer: quoi)

Exercise 4 — Dislocate it

Supply the resumptive pronoun.

  • Mon frère, _____ habite à Lyon. (Answer: il)
  • Ce film, je _____ ai déjà vu. (Answer: l’|l')
  • Le fromage, j’_____ mange peu. (Answer: en)
  • À Paris, je n’_____ vais jamais. (Answer: y)
