🇫🇷 Lesson 18: Le Français des Affaires

B2 taught you the formal letter and the CV. This lesson covers what happens after you are hired: the meeting, the negotiation, and the two written genres that structure French professional life — the compte rendu and the rapport.

The cultural half matters as much as the vocabulary. A French meeting is more argumentative and more hierarchical than a North American one, and mistaking debate for hostility — or informality for equality — causes more trouble than any grammatical error.

💡 Disagreement is participation

In a French meeting, contradicting a proposal is how you show you have read it. Je ne suis pas d’accord, et voici pourquoi is normal and expected; silence can read as either assent or disengagement, neither of which you may intend. The polite forms — je nuancerais, je verrais les choses autrement, permettez-moi d’en douter — soften the delivery, not the disagreement.

📝 Le Compte Rendu

The compte rendu reports what was discussed; the relevé de décisions lists only outcomes. They are different documents and mixing them is the commonest mistake.

Compte renduRelevé de décisions
Narrates the discussionLists only decisions
Past tenses, impersonalPresent or infinitive, with owners
Il a été rappelé que le budget était contraint.Décision : réduire le périmètre. Responsable : M. Dubois. Échéance : 15/06.
No opinions of the authorNo discussion at all

The register of a compte rendu is impersonal past: il a été décidé, les participants ont convenu, la question a été soulevée. You do not appear in it, even if you wrote it.

📊 Le Rapport

Three movements, and the third is the one that gets read.

SectionContains
Le contextewhy the report exists, the remit, the method
Le constatthe findings, with figures, without interpretation
Les préconisationsnumbered, actionable recommendations with owners and costs

A synthèse or note de synthèse at the front — half a page, standing alone — is expected for anything over a few pages. Recommendations that cannot be acted on are the classic failure: il faudrait améliorer la communication is not a préconisation; mettre en place une revue mensuelle de portefeuille, pilotée par la direction financière, à compter de septembre is.

Note je dois en référer. In a hierarchical culture, the person across the table may genuinely not have the authority to close, and saying so is not a delaying tactic. Asking early who decides saves a meeting.

French corporate speech borrows heavily from English and then conjugates it (staffer, forwarder, briefer, updater). Use enough to be understood, not enough to be laughable — and be aware that in Québec the same words are often frowned on where France shrugs.

🪜 Reading the Hierarchy

  • Titles matter. Monsieur le Directeur, Madame la Présidente in writing; le DG, le N+1, un cadre, un cadre dirigeant in speech.
  • The cadre / non-cadre divide is a real legal and social category in France, affecting contract, pension and working time — not just a job label.
  • Vouvoiement is the workplace default in France outside start-ups and creative sectors; in Québec, tu is normal much further up the hierarchy.
  • Decisions travel up. Expect a proposal to be validated at a level above the room.
  • The lunch is part of the work. A two-hour déjeuner d’affaires where business is discussed for fifteen minutes is not a waste of time; it is the relationship-building the deal rests on.

🇫🇷 France

French professional culture values structured argument, written traces and formal hierarchy. Meetings can be long and openly contested; the compte rendu is what settles what actually happened. Email register stays fairly formal even internally — Bonjour Madame, Cordialement — and first names alone in a greeting can read as brusque. The 35-hour framework, RTT days and the droit à la déconnexion shape expectations about response times.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québec's workplace norms are closer to North American ones: flatter hierarchy, earlier tutoiement, shorter meetings, more consensus-seeking and less open contradiction. Vocabulary diverges too — un emploi à temps plein, la CNESST, les vacances rather than les congés, une jobine for a small job — and anglicisms that pass unremarked in Paris (un meeting, un deadline) are often actively replaced in Québec (une réunion, une échéance). A French manager arriving in Montréal usually reads as too direct; a Québécois one in Paris, as insufficiently assertive.

Exercise 1 — Meeting phrases

  1. The agenda is l’ordre du _____. (Answer: jour)
  2. To chair a meeting is _____ une réunion. (Answer: animer)
  3. Politely interrupting: je me _____ de vous interrompre. (Answer: permets)
  4. I would qualify that: je _____. (Answer: nuancerais)
  5. We are off topic: on s’_____ du sujet. (Answer: éloigne|eloigne)

Exercise 2 — Compte rendu or relevé de décisions?

Write CR or RD.

  • « Il a été rappelé que le budget était contraint. » → _____ (Answer: CR)
  • « Décision : réduire le périmètre. Responsable : M. Dubois. » → _____ (Answer: RD)
  • Narrates the discussion in the past → _____ (Answer: CR)
  • Lists owners and deadlines only → _____ (Answer: RD)

Exercise 3 — The three sections of a rapport

  1. Why the report exists and how it was made: le _____ (Answer: contexte)
  2. The findings, with figures: le _____ (Answer: constat)
  3. The numbered actionable recommendations: les _____ (Answer: préconisations|preconisations)
  4. The half-page standalone summary at the front: la _____ (Answer: synthèse|synthese)

Exercise 4 — Jargon into plain French

  • un livrable → ce qu’on doit _____ (Answer: livrer)
  • challenger une proposition → la _____ en question (Answer: remettre)
  • un point bloquant → un _____ (Answer: obstacle)
  • impacter le projet → avoir un _____ sur le projet (Answer: effet)
