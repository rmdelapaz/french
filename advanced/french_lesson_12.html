🇫🇷 Lesson 12: Francophonie Africaine, Maghrébine & Créole

Here is the fact that reorganises everything you think about this language: most French speakers are African, and the proportion is rising fast. Kinshasa is already the largest French-speaking city in the world by some counts; Abidjan, Dakar, Casablanca and Yaoundé are all major centres of French-language life. The demographic centre of French left Europe some time ago.

Lesson 11 covered French's European varieties. This one covers the rest of the world outside Québec — and its point is not a list of quaint words. It is that these are endogenous norms: varieties that developed in place, are used by educated speakers in formal settings, and are not attempts at Parisian French that fell short.

🇲🇦 The Maghreb: Diglossia and Code-Switching

In Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia, French is not official but is everywhere in higher education, business, science and the professions. The result is a functional division and constant switching, often mid-sentence:

Je dois déposer le dossier, inchallah demain, mais le guichet ferme à midi.

ConceptTypical pattern
Home, emotion, humourdarija (Moroccan/Algerian Arabic) or Amazigh
Administration, technology, businessFrench
Religion, formal writtenliterary Arabic
Conversation among graduatesfluid switching between all of them

Maghrebi French also has its own vocabulary — un taxieur, une crémerie for a corner café, faire le ramadan — and a distinctive prosody. And the loans run both ways: French toubib, bled, kif-kif, maboul, clebs and seum all came from Arabic through this contact.

🌴 Creoles Are Not Varieties of French

This distinction matters and is often got wrong. A French-based creole — Haitian, Martinican, Mauritian, Réunion — is a separate language, with its own grammar, its own orthography and (for Haitian) official status and a literature. Its lexicon is largely French-derived, which makes it look approachable and is exactly what misleads people.

Haitian creoleFrenchEnglish
Kijan ou ye ?Comment vas-tu ?How are you?
Mwen pa konprann.Je ne comprends pas.I don’t understand.
Nou prale lakay.Nous allons à la maison.We’re going home.
Li te manje.Il a mangé.He ate.

Notice what is structurally different: no verb conjugation (tense is marked by a separate particle te), no grammatical gender, no articles as you know them, and the definite article follows the noun. The words are French-derived; the language is not French.

Acadian French is not Québécois with an accent — it descends from a different wave of settlement, retains features lost elsewhere (the -ont third-person plural, ils mangeont), and chiac is a distinct contact variety now carried by its own music and literature.

🗣️ Insécurité Linguistique

The term names something real: speakers of a non-metropolitan variety who believe their own French is defective. It is a documented phenomenon in Belgium, Québec, Africa and Acadia alike, and it has consequences — people fall silent, or over-correct, or abandon their variety in formal settings.

The counter-argument, made forcefully by African and Caribbean writers from Senghor to Césaire and now by linguists across the francophonie: French belongs to everyone who speaks it, and an endogenous norm needs no permission from Paris. Kourouma's novels, Rabemananjara's essays and the entire littérature-monde movement are that argument in practice.

🇫🇷 France

France's relationship with African French is complicated: metropolitan speech absorbs African and Maghrebi vocabulary continuously (seum, wesh, bled, kiffer, enjailler), often via music and via the banlieues, while institutional attitudes have been slower to recognise African norms as norms. The Dictionnaire des francophones, launched in 2021, was an explicit attempt to describe the whole francophone lexicon rather than the Parisian one.

🇨🇦 Canada

Canada is itself a multi-variety francophone country, and Québec's own experience of linguistic insecurity has made it unusually attentive to the question. Montréal now has substantial Haitian, Maghrebi and West African French-speaking communities, and their varieties are audible in Québécois media and music. Outside Québec, Acadian and Franco-Ontarian French survive as minority varieties with active institutions, and chiac has gone from stigma to cultural export in a generation.
