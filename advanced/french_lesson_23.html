🇫🇷 Lesson 23: Langue, Identité & Institutions

No other major language is argued about quite like French. Who owns it, who may change it, whether it is in danger, whether autrice is a word, whether le week-end is a defeat — these are live public controversies with laws attached, and at C2 you are expected to be able to hold a position in them, in French, without embarrassment.

This lesson is the map of that terrain. It is also the practical lesson in which you decide what norm you write, and why.

🏛️ L’Académie Française: What It Can and Cannot Do

Founded in 1635, forty members (les Immortels), elected for life. Its mission is to produce the Dictionnaire de l’Académie française and to rule on good usage.

CanCannot
Publish a dictionary and issue opinionsCompel anyone to follow them
Carry enormous symbolic authorityOverrule a law or a ministry
Endorse or reject a spelling reformStop a word entering usage
Shape what teachers and editors believe is correctMove faster than the language — its ninth edition took 89 years

What actually decides French usage, in order of practical weight: usage itself, then commercial dictionaries (Le Robert, Larousse), then the state's commissions de terminologie whose coinages become mandatory in official texts, then the Académie, then the style guides of the press. That ordering is worth stating, because learners routinely overestimate the Académie's power and underestimate Le Robert's.

Note what the law does not do: it does not regulate private speech, it does not ban English words from conversation, and it does not apply outside France. It regulates the state, commerce and employment — which is why English advertising slogans in France always carry an asterisk and a French translation in small print.

♀️ Feminisation and Écriture Inclusive

The most active current debate, and one where French and Québec have taken visibly different paths. Three distinct things are often bundled under one label, and a C2 speaker should keep them apart.

DeviceExampleAcceptance
Feminised job titlesune autrice, une professeure, une chercheuse, une mairestandard in Québec since the 1980s; now widespread in France
Doubletsles étudiants et les étudiantesbroadly accepted, including officially
Point médianles étudiant·e·scontested; banned in French schools and official texts
Epicene rewordingle corps enseignant, la population étudianteuncontroversial and often the best solution
Proximity agreementles hommes et les femmes sont bellesa historical rule, revived as a proposal

💡 The practical advice

Feminised titles are safe and increasingly expected: write une autrice, une professeure, une écrivaine. Doublets are safe in most contexts. The point médian is a political statement and is prohibited in French official and school texts — know that before you use it in an exam or a job application. Epicene rewording achieves the same inclusion with none of the friction, which is why professional editors reach for it first.

🌐 Franglais and the Coinage Machine

France's terminology commissions coin French alternatives, publish them in the Journal officiel, and make them mandatory in official texts. The record is instructive: the successes are the ones that filled a genuine gap, the failures are the ones that fought a word people already liked.

Official coinageForOutcome
un ordinateurcomputertotal success — no rival

le numériquedigitalsuccess

un baladeurwalkmansuccess, then obsolete

un jeu vidéo d’ambiancecasual gamefailure

And the counter-fact worth carrying into any franglais argument: English borrowed massively from French for six centuries (government, justice, beauty, peace, roughly a third of the English lexicon), and French absorbed Frankish, Arabic and Italian without ceasing to be French (lesson 14). Borrowing is how languages work; the interesting question is not whether but which and why.

Québec's situation is structurally different from France's: French there is a minority language on an English-speaking continent, so language policy is a question of survival rather than of prestige. That explains both the interventionism and the coinage productivity — courriel, clavardage, pourriel and baladodiffusion were all Québécois first, and France adopted some of them afterwards.

🌍 Who Owns French?

The question that closes the loop opened in lessons 11 and 12. With most speakers outside France, the claim that Paris sets the norm is increasingly hard to sustain, and the institutions reflect that.

The positions you should be able to state fairly, because you will meet all three:

  • Le purisme — a shared standard is what keeps French usable across continents; without a norm, the language fragments.
  • Le descriptivisme — the standard is an abstraction from usage, and usage has changed; the dictionary follows speakers, not the other way round.
  • La revendication francophone — an African, Caribbean or Québécois speaker's French is not a deviation from Paris; norms are legitimately plural.

🌟 If you want to go further

Read the Académie's own déclaration on écriture inclusive, then the response from a Québécois or Belgian linguist. Both are online, both are written in beautiful soutenu French, and the disagreement is instructive in itself — it is a perfect specimen of the argumentation you practised in lesson 4 and the rhetoric of lesson 19, deployed on a subject the writers genuinely care about.

🎯 Choosing Your Own Norm

You now have to decide, and a C2 speaker should be able to defend the choice.

  • Be consistent within a document. Traditional or rectified spelling, feminised titles or not, courriel or e-mail — pick one and hold it.
  • Match the reader, not your preference. A Québec employer expects courriel and une professeure. A French legal filing expects conservative usage and no point médian.
  • Formal writing is the most uniform register there is. Across France, Belgium, Switzerland, Québec and Dakar, a formal letter differs in almost nothing but institutional nouns.
  • Own your accent. There is no neutral French accent, and trying to erase yours is a waste of the effort better spent on register, precision and range.

🇫🇷 France

France's language politics run through the state: the Académie, the terminology commissions, the loi Toubon, and a constitution whose Article 2 says la langue de la République est le français — which is also why France has never ratified the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, and why the status of Breton, Occitan, Corsican and Alsatian remains contested. The écriture inclusive debate has reached the level of ministerial circulars banning the point médian in schools.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québec's policy is protective rather than prestige-driven, and it is politically central in a way France's is not: Bill 101 and its successors regulate signage, schooling and workplace language, and every revision is a major political event. Québec also moved earlier on feminisation and on coining alternatives to anglicisms. Outside Québec, francophone minorities fight a different battle — for schools, services and broadcasting — and le droit à la langue there means access rather than purity.
