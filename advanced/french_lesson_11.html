🇫🇷 Lesson 11: La Francophonie Européenne

This course has compared France and Québec in every lesson since A1. That comparison has quietly taught you something important — that French has more than one centre — but it has also left a gap. Between Paris and Montréal there are four million francophone Belgians, two million francophone Swiss, a bilingual Grand Duchy, an Italian Alpine valley, and the largest concentration of professional French-writing in the world: the European institutions.

None of these speak "French with mistakes in it". They have standard varieties, with their own dictionaries, their own official terminology and their own unremarkable usages. A C1 speaker recognises them.

Geneva and Neuchâtel say quatre-vingts like France; Lausanne says huitante. Brussels says quatre-vingts too — Belgium innovated on 70 and 90 but not on 80. The forms are historically older than France's, not newer: septante and nonante are regular Latin-derived numerals, and it is France's vigesimal quatre-vingts that is the oddity.

This is the single most practical thing on this page. On dîne à midi in Brussels, Geneva and Montréal; in Paris it means the evening. An invitation for souper is not a late snack.

⚠️ The one that causes real confusion

Belgian savoir can mean pouvoir. Je ne sais pas venir demain in Brussels means I can't come tomorrow, not I don't know how to come. Dutch kunnen covers both senses, and the contact has left its mark.

🏛️ Institutional Vocabulary — Where the Real Differences Live

Vocabulary for everyday objects varies a little. Vocabulary for the state varies a lot, because the states are different.

Note le collège: in France it is lower secondary, in Switzerland it is upper secondary, in Québec it is post-secondary (the cégep system), and in Belgium it can be a private secondary school. One word, four education systems.

🇪🇺 Le Français des Institutions Européennes

Brussels, Luxembourg and Strasbourg generate an enormous quantity of French written largely by non-native speakers and by translators. It is its own register, and if you read EU documents you will meet it constantly.

🌟 If you want to go further

Read the same news story on RTBF (Belgium), RTS (Switzerland), France Info and Radio-Canada. The events will be identical; the numerals, the meal names, the institutional nouns and the register will not. Fifteen minutes of that is worth a chapter of any textbook on variation.

🎯 Which Norm Should You Aim For?

There is no neutral international French, and pretending otherwise is a mistake learners make for years. In practice:

  • Writing formally — the differences nearly vanish. Written standard French is remarkably uniform across all these countries; only institutional nouns differ, and those are dictated by the subject.
  • Speaking — pick the variety of the place you actually deal with, and be consistent. Mixing septante with a Parisian accent is not wrong, but it will be remarked on.
  • Listening — you do not get to choose. Comprehension across varieties is a C1 descriptor, and it is the half of this lesson that matters.

🇫🇷 France

French speakers are often genuinely unaware of the status of these varieties, and septante is frequently heard as quaint rather than standard. France's centralised norm — Paris, the Académie, national broadcast — gives metropolitan French an implicit prestige that Belgians and Swiss notice and sometimes resent. It also means French speakers get less exposure to variation than Belgians or Swiss, who consume French media from France daily.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québec shares with Belgium and Switzerland the experience of being a French-speaking society outside France, and shares specific usages with them — souper, déjeuner for breakfast, and a preference for translating anglicisms rather than importing them. Québec's relationship to the metropolitan norm has been explicitly debated since the 1960s, and Québécois dictionaries such as Usito now describe Québec usage as standard in its own right, exactly as Belgian and Swiss references do.
