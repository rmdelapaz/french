🇫🇷 Lesson 22: Traduire : les Intraduisibles

Translating is not a test of vocabulary. It is the C2 skill that exposes everything else: whether you have understood the register, whether you can restructure a sentence rather than transpose it, and whether you know where the two languages genuinely fail to overlap.

It is also a practical skill you will be asked for — by colleagues, by friends, by employers — long before anyone asks you to write a dissertation. This lesson gives you the professional toolkit, the French names for its moves, and the traps that survive to this level.

🧰 The Seven Procedures

The classical taxonomy (Vinay and Darbelnet, and still the framework French translation schools teach) names seven moves. The first three stay close to the source; the last four rewrite.

FrenchPronunciationWhat it does
l’empruntlahn-PRUHNkeep the foreign word — le week-end
le calqueluh KAHLKtranslate the structure literally — gratte-ciel
la traduction littéralelah trah-dük-syohn lee-tay-RAHLword for word, where it happens to work
la transpositionlah trahns-po-zee-SYOHNchange the word class — he swims well → il est bon nageur
la modulationlah mo-dü-lah-SYOHNchange the viewpoint — it's not difficult → c’est facile
l’équivalencelay-kee-vah-LAHNSswap idiom for idiom — it's raining cats and dogs → il pleut des cordes
l’adaptationlah-dahp-tah-SYOHNreplace the cultural referent altogether

Two more that every translator uses and that French names precisely: l’étoffement (adding words French needs — the man in the car → l’homme qui est dans la voiture) and l’allègement (cutting words French does not need).

💡 The manner-verb problem

English packs manner into the verb (stomp, stroll, dash, slam, glare). French usually puts the movement in the verb and the manner in a complement: sortir en claquant la porte, partir en courant, regarder d’un air furieux. If an English verb resists you, split it: what is the motion, and what is the manner?

🌫️ Les Intraduisibles

Words that do not fail to translate because French is richer, but because they bundle a concept English distributes differently. The professional answer is usually to borrow or gloss, not to force an equivalent.

le dépaysementluh day-pay-eez-MAHNthe pleasant disorientation of being elsewhere

The traffic runs the other way too. French has no economical equivalent for English accountability, commitment in its business sense, serendipity, privacy or to challenge — which is exactly why French business speech has borrowed challenger and why la vie privée is a phrase rather than a word.

🎚️ Register Is the Hardest Part

The commonest professional failure is not mistranslating meaning but mistranslating level. Same content, four registers:

EnglishFrench, matched for register
Get out.Dehors ! / Sors !
Please leave.Veuillez sortir.
I'm going to have to ask you to leave.Je vais devoir vous demander de quitter les lieux.
You're out of here.Tu dégages.

Translate the register first, then the words. And remember the asymmetry from lesson 10: English carries politeness in lexical padding (please, sorry, just, I was wondering), French in grammar (conditional, negation, impersonal). A word-for-word translation of English politeness produces French that is either servile or absurd.

🎬 Constraints: Subtitling and Sworn Translation

GenreConstraintConsequence
le sous-titrage~37 characters per line, 2 lines, reading speedmust compress; loses redundancy, hesitation, register markers
le doublagelip-sync and durationrewrites lines to fit the mouth
la traduction assermentéelegal fidelity, sworn translatorcannot paraphrase; awkwardness is acceptable, invention is not
la localisationcultural adaptationchanges referents, units, names, examples
la traduction littérairevoice and effectthe freest — and the hardest to judge

Knowing which constraint you are under is half the job. A subtitler who translates faithfully produces unreadable subtitles; a sworn translator who translates elegantly produces an invalid document.

🌟 If you want to go further

Take a page of a novel you know in both languages and compare them line by line, naming the procedure the translator used at each divergence — transposition ici, modulation là, étoffement là. This is exactly the exercise French translation schools set, and it teaches more about both languages than any amount of vocabulary drilling.

🇫🇷 France

France has a strong tradition of literary translation as an authored activity — translators are named on covers, reviewed, and prized (the prix Nerval, the prix Pierre-François Caillé). The Vinay-and-Darbelnet framework above remains the pedagogical standard in French translation schools such as the ESIT and the ISIT. Retranslation is normal and respected: major foreign novels are re-translated every generation, and the differences are publicly debated.

🇨🇦 Canada

Canada is one of the world's great translation economies — federal bilingualism means everything is produced in two languages, and Canada trains more translators per capita than almost anywhere. That has real linguistic consequences: Québécois French is under constant translation pressure, which is why the OQLF's Banque de dépannage linguistique and Termium exist and why Québec polices calques so actively. Canadian translators also produce tradaptation for theatre, and Michel Tremblay's own translations of Québécois plays into other varieties are a case study in the problem this lesson describes.

Exercise 1 — Name the procedure

Write transposition, modulation, équivalence or emprunt.

  1. he swims well → il est bon nageur → _____ (Answer: transposition)
  2. it's not difficult → c’est facile → _____ (Answer: modulation)
  3. it's raining cats and dogs → il pleut des cordes → _____ (Answer: équivalence|equivalence)
  4. the weekend → le week-end → _____ (Answer: emprunt)

Exercise 2 — Faux amis

Give the correct French for the English word.

  • actually → _____ (Answer: en fait)
  • eventually → _____ (Answer: finalement)
  • to realise (understand) → se rendre _____ (Answer: compte)
  • to support (a cause) → _____ (Answer: soutenir)
  • evidence → des _____ (Answer: preuves)

Exercise 3 — Split the manner verb

  1. She stormed out. → Elle est sortie en _____ la porte. (Answer: claquant)
  2. He ran off. → Il est parti en _____. (Answer: courant)
  3. He drove there. → Il s’y est rendu en _____. (Answer: voiture)
  4. She glared at him. → Elle l’a regardé d’un _____ furieux. (Answer: air)

Exercise 4 — Match the register

  • Get out! (shouted) → _____ ! (Answer: dehors|sors)
  • Please leave. (formal notice) → _____ sortir. (Answer: veuillez)
  • I'm going to have to ask you to leave. → Je vais devoir vous demander de _____ les lieux. (Answer: quitter)
  • French carries politeness mainly in its _____ rather than in extra words. (Answer: grammaire|grammar)
