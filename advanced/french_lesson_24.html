🇫🇷 Lesson 24: DALF C1 & C2 + Plan de Maîtrise

The last lesson of the course. No new grammar — there is none left to teach. This is about proving what you now know, and then about keeping it.

One thing here genuinely is new material rather than revision: the synthèse de documents. It is a taught French genre with a fixed method, it is half of the DALF C1 written paper, and nobody produces a good one by instinct.

⚠️ The note éliminatoire is where candidates lose

You can score well overall and still fail on one weak paper. Since the four C1 papers are separately eliminating, the rational preparation strategy is to raise your weakest skill, not to polish your strongest. Most candidates do the opposite because it is more enjoyable.

📑 La Synthèse de Documents — the Method

You receive two or three documents on one theme, roughly 1000 to 1200 words in total, and produce a single coherent text of about 220 words (C1) that presents what they collectively say. Three absolute rules:

  • No personal opinion. Not a sentence of it. This is the rule most often broken.
  • No copied wording. Reformulation is what is being marked. Lifting a phrase from a source loses points even if it is accurate.
  • No document-by-document structure. "Document 1 says… Document 2 says…" is an automatic low mark. The synthesis must be organised by theme.

The five steps

StepTimeWhat you do
1. Read for the shared question10 minWhat single issue are all the documents about?
2. Build a grid15 minThemes down the side, documents across the top. Fill the cells.
3. Order the themes5 minTwo or three themes, in a logical order — that is your plan
4. Write30 minIntro (the theme and the sources), body by theme, short close
5. Check10 minWord count, no opinion, no lifted phrases, agreements

The grid is the whole technique. Once the cells are filled, the convergences and disagreements between the documents are visible on the page, and you write across the rows rather than down the columns.

✍️ L’Essai Argumenté

The second half of the C1 written paper: around 250 words stating and defending a position, usually addressed to a specific reader (a newspaper, a committee, a director). It is the dissertation of lesson 16 in miniature, plus a register requirement.

DoDon’t
Announce a clear position earlyBalance both sides and never commit
Concede then rebut (lesson 4)Ignore the opposing case
Match the register to the named readerWrite the same register for a minister and a friend
Use two or three well-developed argumentsList six shallow ones
Respect the word count within 10 per centWrite 400 words because you had more to say

🗣️ The Oral

StageC1
Preparation1 hour with a dossier of documents
Exposé8–10 minutes, structured, on a problématique you formulate
Discussionthe examiner challenges your position for 15–20 minutes

Two things matter more than content. The plan is visible — announce it and signal each transition aloud, exactly as in a written dissertation. And defend without rigidity: when the examiner presses you, conceding a genuine point and then qualifying it scores better than either collapsing or repeating yourself. That is lesson 4 and lesson 19 being examined directly.

⏱️ Timing and Tactics

PaperTactic
Compréhension oraleRead the questions first. Take notes in the gaps, not while a key sentence is being spoken.
Compréhension écriteLocate the answer, then read that paragraph closely. Do not read the whole text twice.
Production écriteSplit the 2h30 deliberately: 1h10 synthèse, 1h10 essai, 10 min final check. Never leave one unfinished.
Production oraleSpend the first 20 of your 60 preparation minutes on the problématique and plan, not on notes.
All papersAnswer something for every task. An unattempted section is where the note éliminatoire comes from.

♾️ Le Plan de Maîtrise

The exam is a milestone, not a destination. Mastery is a maintenance problem: a language you do not use degrades, and it degrades from the top — the soutenu register and the rare vocabulary go first.

FrequencyPractice
Daily15 minutes of real reading — press, a novel, anything not written for learners
DailySomething in your ears: a podcast, the radio, a series without subtitles
WeeklyWrite 300 words that someone will read — a comment, a letter, a review
WeeklyOne real conversation, ideally one where you have to argue
MonthlyOne difficult text you would not normally choose — a legal document, a poem, a philosophy essay
QuarterlyReread this tier's cheat sheet and check the can-do list for slippage
YearlySomething long and ambitious: a novel in a register you find hard, or a course in French on a subject that is not French

💡 The single most effective habit

Use French for something that is not French. Follow a subject you care about — cooking, law, astronomy, football tactics — in French because that is where the good sources are. Language studied as a subject plateaus; language used as a tool does not.

🎉 Where You Started, and Where You Are

Seventy-eight lessons ago, this course opened with bonjour. Since then you have built the sentence (A1–A2), the paragraph and the argument (B1–B2), and in this tier the register, the variety and the precision (C1–C2). You can read a nineteenth-century novel and scan its verse. You can follow a Québécois news bulletin, an Ivorian song and a Swiss referendum debate. You can write a mise en demeure, a synthèse, a compte rendu and a joke.

The remaining work is not upward — there is no C3. It is outward, and it is the enjoyable part: more books, more places, more people, more of the language's enormous back catalogue. You are no longer learning French. You are using it.

Bonne route, et bon vent.

🇫🇷 France

The DALF is administered by France Éducation international through the Institut français and Alliance française network, and a C1 is the standard language requirement for French university admission — often accepted in place of the TCF. It is valid for life, which the TCF and TEF are not. Registration is per-session and per-centre, so check dates several months ahead.

🇨🇦 Canada

For Canadian immigration purposes the DALF is generally not the test that counts: IRCC requires the TEF Canada or TCF Canada, scored on the NCLC scale rather than the CEFR, and those results expire after two years. Québec's own programmes accept a range of proofs including the TEFAQ. If your goal is Canada, take the DALF for yourself and the TEF for the file — and note that the TEF's oral is a quite different format from the DALF's exposé, so it needs its own practice.
