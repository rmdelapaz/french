🇫🇷 Lesson 13: Humour, Jeux de Mots & Second Degré

You can hold a serious conversation in French. The moment you cannot handle is the other one: the table where everyone is saying the opposite of what they mean, a pun has just been made on a word you know, and the appropriate response is a counter-joke within two seconds.

Humour is the last thing to arrive in a foreign language, and it is genuinely a language skill rather than a personality trait. This lesson covers the mechanics — the second degré, the pun forms French prizes, the satirical tradition — and the social rules about who you may laugh at.

🎭 Le Second Degré

The defining feature of French wit. Au premier degré = taken literally. Au second degré = the speaker means the opposite, or is quoting a stupidity in order to mock it. Missing it is the classic foreigner's error, and being told c’était du second degré is mildly humiliating for everyone.

How to hear it. French irony is often marked by flatness rather than exaggeration — a deliberately level delivery, sometimes with a very slight slowing. English irony tends to over-signal with pitch; French frequently under-signals on purpose, which is why it goes past learners. Context and plausibility are your real cues: if a statement is too stupid to be sincere, it is not sincere.

🔤 Le Calembour: Punning Is a Prestige Form

This is a real cultural difference. In English, a pun draws a groan. In French, a good calembour draws admiration — it is a demonstration of command over the language, and French is unusually rich in homophones, which makes the game easier and more valued.

PunHow it works
Il n’y a que maille qui m’aille. (knitting shop)maille (stitch) / m’aille (suits me)
Entre deux mots, il faut choisir le moindre.maux (ills) / mots (words) — Valéry
La vie est un long fleuve tranquille… et moi je rame.literal/figurative switch
Un boulanger qui fait des pains… et des misères.faire des pains / faire des misères
Les vieux motards que j’aimais. (= les vieux motards que j’aimais)homophone of les vieilles motos que j’aimais — a contrepèterie-style trap

Puns are also the standard register of French newspaper headlines and shop names. A hairdresser called Tif Annie (= Tiphanie, from tif = hair), a cheese shop called La Ferme de mes rêves — once you notice, every high street is a pun.

🔄 La Contrepèterie

A French speciality with no real English equivalent: swap sounds between two words so that a second, usually indecent, reading appears. The convention is never to explain it — je vous laisse méditer là-dessus.

The polite specimen everyone quotes: Femme folle à la messe → swap and you get femme molle à la fesse. Le Canard enchaîné has run a weekly column of them (Sur l’album de la comtesse) since 1951, and readers compete to decode them.

💡 Why this matters beyond the joke

Contrepèteries are a phonological workout. Decoding one requires you to hold a French phrase in memory as sound rather than as spelling and manipulate it. If you can do that, your listening has genuinely improved.

The social rule. Teasing is a marker of intimacy and it must be reciprocal. If someone charrie you, the correct response is to give as good back, not to apologise or to bristle. If you are not yet close enough to be teased back, do not tease first. And the universally safe joke is against yourself: l’autodérision reads as confidence in French, not as weakness.

🗡️ La Satire

France has an institutional satirical tradition that is older and more legally protected than most. Knowing the landmarks is part of following the news.

InstitutionWhat it is
Le Canard enchaînéweekly satirical paper since 1915; carries real investigative scoops alongside wordplay
Charlie Hebdosatirical weekly; deliberately transgressive, at the centre of the free-speech debate
Les Guignols de l’infopuppet news satire that shaped French political humour from 1988
La chanson engagéeBrassens, Brel, Renaud — satire as song
Le stand-upfrom Coluche to today; the cultural centre has shifted towards it

Three names to know, because they are quoted constantly: Raymond Devos (absurdist wordplay, dazzling and very learnable — his sketches are almost a grammar lesson), Pierre Desproges (black humour, elaborate soutenu syntax deployed for insult), Coluche (working-class satire; also founded the Restos du cœur).

And the register warning: French humour includes a robust tradition of the taboo and the offensive, and what circulates publicly in France may not travel. Understanding a joke and repeating it are different decisions.

💬 Joining In

Some usable moves, in ascending order of risk:

  • Acknowledge the joke — Ah, très bien vu. / Elle est bonne, celle-là.
  • Escalate the absurdity — take their exaggeration one step further. This is the most reliable way in.
  • Self-deprecate — Ça, venant de moi, ça ne m’étonne pas.
  • Flag your own irony if it fails — C’était du second degré, hein.
  • Deadpan — high reward, hard to land, because the flat delivery has to be convincing in a second language.

🌟 If you want to go further

Watch one Raymond Devos sketch with the transcript in front of you — Où courent-ils ? or Parler pour ne rien dire. Every laugh is built on a grammatical or lexical ambiguity you have studied in this course. It is the best comprehension exercise in French, and it is funnier than any textbook.

🇫🇷 France

French humour targets politics, religion and social pretension, and legally protected satire is a point of national self-image. Second degré is so dominant that sincerity often needs marking (je te dis ça sérieusement). Word-play is prestige: a well-turned calembour in a meeting raises your standing rather than lowering it.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québécois humour is a major industry — the Just for Laughs / Juste pour rire festival, a dense stand-up scene, and the tradition of the monologuiste from Yvon Deschamps onwards. The taboos differ: Québec swears with religious vocabulary (the sacres: tabarnak, câlice, ostie) where France swears with the body, so a Québécois joke can sound blasphemous to a French ear and a French one merely crude to a Québécois one. Québec also mines its relationship with France and with English Canada for comedy in a way France has no equivalent of.

Exercise 3 — Name the form

  • A friend teases you. The right response is to tease _____ (back / apologise). (Answer: back)
