🇫🇷 Lesson 4: Nouns, Gender & Articles

🧩 Why Articles Are the Whole Ballgame

In English you can say bread and be done with it. In French you almost never say a bare noun — you say the bread, a bread, or some bread, and the little word in front changes depending on the noun's gender, its number, and whether you mean all of it, one of it, or part of it.

Think of a French noun as a passenger who is never allowed to travel alone. It always brings a small companion — an article — and that companion announces two things before the noun even arrives: is this masculine or feminine? and how much of it are we talking about? Learn the companion and the rest of the language falls into place, because adjectives, pronouns and past participles all take their cue from it.

💡 Learn the Article With the Word

Never memorise table. Memorise la table. Never memorise livre. Memorise le livre. The article costs you one extra syllable and saves you a thousand corrections later. Every vocabulary list in this course gives you the article for exactly this reason.

⚖️ Gender: Every Noun Is Masculine or Feminine

French nouns have a grammatical gender. For people it often matches biology (un homme, une femme), but for objects it is simply an inherited label: a table is feminine, a book is masculine, and no amount of reasoning will explain why.

⚠️ Gender Is Not Optional Decoration

Gender is not just about le and la. It travels through the whole sentence: C'est un petit livre vert but C'est une petite maison verte. Getting the article right is what makes the adjective, the pronoun and later the past participle come out right too.

🔑 The Three Article Families

French has three sets of articles. Each answers a different question about the noun.

FamilyMasculineFemininePluralMeans roughly
Definitelelalesthe / in general
Indefiniteununedesa, an / some (countable)
Partitivedude ladessome / an unmeasured amount

1. Definite: le, la, les, l'

Use the definite article for a specific thing and — unlike English — for whole categories and for likes and dislikes.

FrenchPronunciationMeaning
Le livre est sur la table.luh LEEVR ay sür lah TABLThe book is on the table. (specific)
J'aime le café.zhem luh kah-FAYI like coffee. (the category, all coffee)
Les Français aiment le pain.lay frahn-SAY em luh PANFrench people love bread. (generalisation)
L'hôtel est fermé.loh-TEL ay fair-MAYThe hotel is closed. (l' before a vowel or silent h)

✅ The Elision Rule

Le and la both shrink to l' in front of a vowel sound or a silent h: l'ami, l'école, l'heure, l'homme. French hates two vowel sounds bumping into each other — this is the same instinct behind j'ai, d'accord and qu'est-ce que.

2. Indefinite: un, une, des

Use these for countable things you could point at one of: un café (one cup of coffee), une pomme (an apple), des amis (some friends).

FrenchPronunciationMeaning
Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.zhuh voo-DRAY uhn kah-FAYI'd like a coffee, please.
Elle a une soeur et des cousins.el ah ün SUHR ay day koo-ZANShe has a sister and some cousins.
Il y a des étudiants dans la classe.eel ee ah day-zay-tü-dee-AHNThere are (some) students in the class.

⚠️ English Drops "Some" — French Never Does

English happily says "I have friends in Paris." French requires the article: J'ai des amis à Paris. A bare plural noun is one of the most recognisable beginner mistakes, and it is entirely avoidable.

3. Partitive: du, de la, de l', des

This family has no clean English equivalent. Use it for an unmeasured part of something — the stuff you can't count individually: water, bread, patience, money, time.

FrenchPronunciationMeaning
Je mange du pain.zhuh mahnzh dü PANI'm eating (some) bread.
Elle boit de l'eau.el bwah duh LOHShe's drinking (some) water.
Nous avons de la chance.noo-zah-VOHN duh lah SHAHNSWe're lucky. (lit. we have some luck)
Tu veux du fromage ?tü vuh dü fro-MAHZHDo you want some cheese?

💡 One Noun, Three Articles, Three Meanings

J'aime le café. — I like coffee (in general).
Je voudrais un café. — I'd like a (cup of) coffee.
Je bois du café. — I drink (some) coffee.
Same noun, three completely different messages. This one trio, repeated aloud, teaches the whole system.

🚫 The Big Twist: de After Negation and Quantity

Here is the rule that separates learners who sound French from learners who don't. After a negative and after a quantity word, the indefinite and partitive articles collapse into plain de (or d' before a vowel).

PositiveNegativeMeaning
J'ai un frère.Je n'ai pas de frère.I don't have a brother.
Elle mange de la viande.Elle ne mange pas de viande.She doesn't eat meat.
Il y a des chaises.Il n'y a pas de chaises.There aren't any chairs.
Je bois de l'alcool.Je ne bois pas d'alcool.I don't drink alcohol.

🚨 The One Exception You Must Know

After être, the article does not change in the negative: C'est un problème → Ce n'est pas un problème. The de-rule applies to direct objects, not to what something is.

⚠️ The Plural You Hear Is the Article, Not the -s

Final -s is silent, so le livre and les livres differ only in that first little word: luh vs lay. When listening, the article is your only clue to number — which is exactly why French speakers pronounce it so clearly.

🇫🇷🇨🇦 Across the Atlantic

🇫🇷 France

In fast speech, the partitive often gets swallowed: J'veux du pain sounds like "zhvuh dü pan". Some borrowed English words keep a gender that surprises learners — un job, un mail, une start-up.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québécois French often contracts sur la → s'a and dans les → dins in casual speech: s'a table, dins poches. Québec also prefers French-made words where France borrows English — un courriel (not un mail), une fin de semaine (not un week-end) — so the gender you learn may differ by region.

Exercise 1 — Choose the right article

Fill each blank with le, la, les, un, une, des, du, de la or de.

  1. J'aime _____ chocolat. (I like chocolate in general) (Answer: le)
  2. Je voudrais _____ baguette, s'il vous plaît. (one baguette) (Answer: une)
  3. Elle boit _____ eau. (some water — feminine noun starting with a vowel) (Answer: de l'|de l)
  4. Nous mangeons _____ fromage. (some cheese — masculine) (Answer: du)
  5. Il n'y a pas _____ lait dans le frigo. (after a negative) (Answer: de)
  6. _____ voiture de mon père est bleue. (the specific car) (Answer: La)
  7. Tu as beaucoup _____ travail aujourd'hui. (after a quantity word) (Answer: de)
  8. Ils ont _____ amis à Montréal. (some friends — plural, countable) (Answer: des)

Exercise 2 — Make it negative

Rewrite the underlined article as it appears in the negative sentence.

  1. J'ai un chien. → Je n'ai pas _____ chien. (Answer: de)
  2. Elle mange des légumes. → Elle ne mange pas _____ légumes. (Answer: de)
  3. C'est un problème. → Ce n'est pas _____ problème. (careful — this one is the exception) (Answer: un)
  4. Nous buvons du vin. → Nous ne buvons pas _____ vin. (Answer: de)
