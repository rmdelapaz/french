🇫🇷 Lesson 19: The Passé Composé

⏮️ The Tense That Tells What Happened

The passé composé is the everyday past tense of French. It reports completed events: things that started, finished, and can be ticked off a list. J'ai mangé. Elle est partie. Nous avons visité Québec.

It's called "composed" because it is built from two pieces, exactly like English "I have eaten":

💡 The Formula

subject + avoir/être (present) + past participle
J' + ai + mangé = J'ai mangé (I ate / I have eaten).
The helper verb carries the person; the participle carries the action. Get the helper right and you're 80% of the way there.

🚨 Être Verbs Make Their Participle Agree

With être, the participle behaves like an adjective — it agrees with the subject:
Il est allé · Elle est allée · Ils sont allés · Elles sont allées.
With avoir, nothing agrees with the subject: Elle a mangé, never mangée.

FrenchPronunciationMeaning

Elle est partie hier.el ay par-TEE ee-AIRShe left yesterday.
Nous sommes arrivés à midi.noo som-zah-ree-VAYWe arrived at noon.

Reflexive Verbs Always Take Être

Je me suis levé(e) · Elle s'est couchée · Nous nous sommes amusés · Ils se sont dépêchés. The reflexive pronoun goes before the helper, and the participle normally agrees with the subject.

⚠️ Six Verbs Can Take Either Helper

Monter, descendre, sortir, rentrer, retourner and passer switch to avoir when they have a direct object, and their meaning shifts:
Je suis sorti. (I went out.) vs J'ai sorti la poubelle. (I took out the bin.)
Elle est montée. (She went up.) vs Elle a monté les valises. (She carried the cases up.)

🚫 Negation and Questions in the Past

The negative sandwich wraps the helper verb, not the participle — the participle stays outside.

💡 One French Past = Three English Pasts

J'ai mangé covers "I ate", "I have eaten" and "I did eat". Don't look for three separate French tenses — the passé composé does all three jobs, and the context supplies the nuance.

🇫🇷🇨🇦 Across the Atlantic

🇫🇷 France

The passé composé rules speech. The passé simple (il mangea) survives only in novels, history books and fairy tales — recognise it when reading, but never use it when speaking.

🇨🇦 Canada

Identical structure, but listen for the helper contracting: J'ai → "j'ai" stays, while tu as → "t'as" and il a → "y'a". You'll hear J'ai-tu dit ça ? with the Québécois question particle, and avoir in places where France says être in very casual speech.

  5. Je me _____ levé à six heures. (reflexive!) (Answer: suis)
