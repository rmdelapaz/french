🇫🇷 Lesson 7: The Present Tense

🔧 How French Verbs Work

Every French verb arrives in its dictionary form — the infinitive — and every infinitive ends in one of three ways: -er (parler), -ir (finir) or -re (vendre). To conjugate, you chop off that ending to get the stem, then bolt on the ending that matches your subject.

Picture it as a plug and a socket. The stem carries the meaning (parl- = speaking), and the ending carries the person (-ons = we). French keeps them strictly separate, which is why once you know the six endings for a group you can conjugate hundreds of verbs you have never seen.

💡 One Present Tense, Three English Translations

Je parle français can mean "I speak French", "I am speaking French" or "I do speak French". French has no separate progressive tense, so one form covers all three. If you must stress that it's happening right now, say Je suis en train de parler.

1️⃣ -ER Verbs — The Huge Group

About 90% of French verbs end in -er, and almost every new verb entering the language joins this group (googliser, télétravailler). Learn this pattern and you have unlocked most of the language.

FrenchPronunciationMeaning

✅ Four of the Six Sound Identical

parle, parles, parle, parlent are all pronounced PARL. Only nous (-ons) and vous (-ez) change the sound. So in speech, the pronoun carries the information — which is why French can never drop the subject the way Spanish or Italian do.

2️⃣ -IR Verbs — The Finir Family

Regular -ir verbs insert -iss- in the plural. It sounds odd until you hear it once: nous finissons.

FrenchPronunciationMeaning
je finiszhuh fee-NEEI finish
tu finistü fee-NEEyou finish
il / elle / on finiteel fee-NEEhe / she finishes
nous finissonsnoo fee-nee-SOHNwe finish
vous finissezvoo fee-nee-SAYyou finish
ils / elles finissenteel fee-NEESthey finish

Same pattern: choisir (to choose), réussir (to succeed), grandir (to grow), réfléchir (to think it over), remplir (to fill in).

⚠️ Not Every -ir Verb Plays by These Rules

Partir, sortir, dormir, venir and ouvrir all end in -ir but follow their own patterns: je pars, je sors, je dors, je viens, j'ouvre. They are common enough that you learn them individually rather than by rule.

💡 Savoir vs Connaître

Savoir = to know a fact or how to do something: Je sais nager. Je sais où il habite.
Connaître = to be acquainted with a person, place or work of art: Je connais Marie. Tu connais Montréal ?
English flattens both into "know", so this is a distinction you must build deliberately.

Two Verbs, One Sentence

When two verbs meet, the first one conjugates and the second stays in the infinitive — no linking word needed.

🇫🇷🇨🇦 Across the Atlantic

🇫🇷 France

Conjugation is identical, but the pronouns shrink in speech: tu as → "t'as", tu es → "t'es", il y a → "y a". You'll hear Tu veux as "tu veux" clearly in the south and as "tveux" in Paris.

🇨🇦 Canada

Québec adds -tu as a yes/no question marker on the conjugated verb: Tu viens-tu ? (Are you coming?), Ça se peut-tu ? (Is that possible?). It's not the pronoun tu — it's a question particle, and it's used with every person: Il vient-tu ?

  2. Nous _____ à Las Vegas. (habiter) (Answer: habitons)

Exercise 2 — -ir and -re verbs

  1. Elle _____ ses devoirs à huit heures. (finir) (Answer: finit)

  3. Vous _____ toujours le menu du jour. (choisir) (Answer: choisissez)
  4. Je _____ mon vélo. (vendre) (Answer: vends)

  4. Je _____ où elle habite. (savoir — a fact) (Answer: sais)
